Week 2, session 2: before the session

Describing data with numbers and graphs, tried out in your browser

Before you test anything, you describe it: how big a typical value is, how much the values vary, and what shape they make. In session 2 you’ll describe a dataset of patients tested for COVID-19, in numbers, in a table and in graphs. This page covers the ideas you’ll need:

  • the mean and the median, and why they can disagree;
  • the standard deviation, a measure of spread;
  • summaries by group, with group_by() and summarise(); and
  • histograms, and choosing the right graph for the question.
NoteThis page takes a little longer to start

Your browser needs to download the dplyr and ggplot2 packages as well as starting R. Give it up to a minute the first time. The box below loads them; it runs by itself once everything is ready.

1. The mean, the median, and one extreme value

Here are the C-reactive protein (CRP) results of seven patients, in mg/L. CRP is a marker of inflammation; in a healthy person it’s usually below about 5 mg/L.

The mean adds up the values and divides by how many there are. Six of the seven patients have a CRP between 4 and 15 mg/L, but the mean is over 50, because the one patient with 310 mg/L pulls it up. The mean is not a good description of a “typical” patient here.

The median is the middle value when the values are sorted: half are below it and half above. A single extreme value doesn’t move it, because it only cares about the order.

Your turn

Calculate the median of crp. (The function is called exactly what you’d guess.)

NoteHint

The function is median().

TipSolution 
median(crp)

When the mean and the median are close, the values are roughly symmetric. When the mean is much bigger than the median, a few high values are stretching the distribution to the right: it is right-skewed. Many biomarkers behave like this, and for them the median is usually the better summary.

2. Spread: the standard deviation

Two groups can have the same mean and still be very different. The standard deviation (SD) measures how spread out the values are: roughly, the typical distance of a value from the mean.

Both groups have a mean of 50, but group B’s values are far more spread out, and its SD is ten times larger. When the data are skewed, the interquartile range (IQR, the range of the middle half of the values) does the same job for the median that the SD does for the mean: IQR(crp).

3. Summaries for each group

summarise() reduces a whole column to a few summary numbers. Put group_by() in front of it, and you get the same summaries for every group. Using ToothGrowth again (the guinea pigs given vitamin C as orange juice, OJ, or ascorbic acid, VC), here is the number of animals and the mean tooth length at each dose:

Read it as: “take ToothGrowth, and then split it into groups by dose, and then calculate, for each group, the number of rows and the mean of len”. The names on the left of each = become the column names of the result.

Your turn

Calculate the mean tooth length for each supplement (supp) instead of each dose. Call the column mean_len.

NoteHint

The grouping column is supp, and the summary is mean(len), just as in the example.

TipSolution 
ToothGrowth |>
  group_by(supp) |>
  summarise(mean_len = mean(len))

4. Seeing the shape: histograms

Numbers like the mean and SD summarise a distribution; a histogram shows it. It divides the range of values into bins and draws a bar for the number of values in each bin. You only need to give it an x:

binwidth = 2 makes each bar 2 units wide. fill and colour are fixed settings for the inside and the outline of the bars, so they go outside aes(). #753bbd is a hex code for a shade of purple.

Your turn

Draw a histogram of tooth length with wider bins, 5 units each. Does the shape look different?

NoteHint

Replace the blank with the number 5.

TipSolution 
ggplot(ToothGrowth, aes(x = len)) +
  geom_histogram(binwidth = 5, fill = "#753bbd", colour = "white")

Which graph?

Each type of graph answers a different kind of question. Decide which you would use for each question below, then open the box to check.

  1. How are patients’ cholesterol levels distributed?
  2. Does systolic blood pressure differ between three treatment groups?
  3. Is there a relationship between age and white cell count?
  4. How many patients in each group are smokers?
  5. How does mean HbA1c change over the six months of a trial?
TipAnswers
  1. A histogram: the distribution of one continuous variable.
  2. Boxplots, one per group: a continuous variable compared across groups.
  3. A scatter plot: two continuous variables, one point per patient.
  4. A bar chart of counts: a categorical variable.
  5. A line graph: change over time.

Before you come in

You’re ready for session 2 if you can:

  • explain the difference between the mean and the median, and why one extreme value affects one much more than the other;
  • explain what the standard deviation tells you;
  • use group_by() and summarise() to calculate a summary for each group; and
  • draw a histogram with geom_histogram(), and say which kind of graph suits which kind of question.

In the session you’ll describe a dataset of 60 patients tested for COVID-19, build a summary table you could put in a report, and draw histograms, boxplots, a scatter plot and a bar chart.
